## Q-learning vs SARSA: rollout divergence (Task A.2)

**Predicted:** SARSA routes around the fire gap at `(4,7)`/`(4,8)` where Q-learning cuts
through it, per `config/level1.json`'s `_design_note`.

**Observed:** `plot_qualitative_rollout()` shows the two greedy (epsilon=0) rollouts are
identical — both take the 18-step route along row 9 and column 9, collecting all three
apples without ever approaching the fire gap. This held even after retraining with a much
slower epsilon decay (6000 episodes, `epsilon_end=0.0`, tested but not committed), so it
is not an under-exploration artifact.

**Why:** the rock wall blocks column `x=4` for rows `y=0..6`; rows `y=7,8` at that column
are fire (instant death); row `y=9` is the only open crossing. For apples at `(9,0)`,
`(9,9)`, `(5,9)` — all reachable via row 9 — the safe crossing is *also* the shortest path.
The fire gap is one row above the only safe route and offers no distance saving, so there
is never a genuine risk/reward trade-off for the agent to resolve, on- or off-policy. This
is a property of the tile layout and apple placement, not of the update rule under test —
`R-ALG-2` (off-policy vs on-policy bootstrap) is still correctly implemented and separately
covered by `tests/test_algorithms.py`; this is a level-design finding, not a bug finding.

**Death rate near convergence** (last 20% of training episodes, still under the training
schedule's own residual `epsilon_end=0.05`, not greedy): Q-learning 4.3%, SARSA 4.8%,
Expected SARSA 5.8% — small, overlapping-noise differences, consistent with the rollout
finding that final policy quality is not meaningfully distinguished by the on/off-policy
choice on this specific level layout.

## Expected SARSA vs the other two (Task A.6)

Expected SARSA was trained for real on level1 (seed 0, same config as Q-learning/SARSA;
`task2_level1_expected_sarsa.csv`, `models/level1_expected_sarsa.json`).

Textbook expectation: Expected SARSA should have *lower* variance than SARSA (it averages
over the epsilon-greedy action distribution instead of sampling one next action) while
remaining on-policy-safe near hazards, at the cost of one extra sum over actions per
update.

Observed here: last-20%-of-training return std is Q-learning 0.595, SARSA 0.619, Expected
SARSA 0.673 — Expected SARSA does *not* show lower variance in this run; if anything it is
slightly higher. Its greedy rollout is identical to the other two (same 18-step safe path).
Two honest explanations, not mutually exclusive: (1) level1 has no fire-adjacent decision
boundary for any of the three algorithms to be more/less conservative around (see above),
so the theoretical variance advantage — which stems from the bootstrap term itself, not the
downstream policy — has little opportunity to matter for the final rollout even if it's
present in the update noise; (2) "last 20% of *training* episodes" still injects
`epsilon_end=0.05`-driven exploration noise into the logged return, which can swamp a small
per-update variance difference. We report the actual numbers rather than asserting the
textbook result held, per this project's evidence standard (`docs/RULES.md` R-EVAL/R-REPORT
rules: quantify, don't assert from a plot alone).
